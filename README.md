# P.L. 119-21 and Title 26: the amendments checked instruction by instruction

Arxo Law · contact: hi@arxo.io

We applied the amendments of P.L. 119-21 (139 Stat. 72) to Title 26 to the Code as it stood
before enactment (OLRC release point 119-12), counting occurrences, and compared the result with
the OLRC releases 119-33 and 119-121. Every item below was checked by hand against the Statutes at
Large page.

Not legal advice. Not affiliated with Congress, the Joint Committee on Taxation, the Office of the
Law Revision Counsel, the Treasury or the IRS.

## Main points

1. **Refundable adoption credit: two section lists were not amended.** §23(a)(4), added by
   §70402(a) (139 Stat. 213), treats up to $5,000 of the credit as a subpart C credit. Section 23
   is not listed in 26 U.S.C. 6211(b)(4)(A), which governs negative amounts of tax for deficiency
   purposes, or in 31 U.S.C. 1324(b)(2), which names the credit provisions paid from the permanent
   refund appropriation. When Congress made §25A partly refundable (2009), moved the adoption credit
   into subpart C as §36C (2010), and made §21 refundable and §24 fully refundable for 2021, it
   amended both lists in each of these cases. H.R. 2833, the Adoption Tax Credit Refundability Act of 2025, would
   have added the credit to both lists (§2(a)(2)(E) and (G)). In 1997 Congress did not, and a technical
   correction closed the gap: the 1997 Act made part of the child tax credit refundable (§24(d))
   without adding it to §6211(b)(4)(A), and P.L. 106-554 (2000) added "24(d)" effective as if included
   in the 1997 Act. On October 16, 2026 the IRS announced
   that it has begun issuing refunds to more than 50,000 taxpayers whose adoption credit
   carryforwards from 2020 through 2024 increase the refundable portion of the credit for 2025
   (IR-2026-123). *Status: the omission is established; its consequences are a question for
   Congress, the Treasury and the IRS.*
2. **Trump accounts: the new additional tax is not excluded from regular tax liability.**
   §530A(d)(5)(C), added by §70204(a) (139 Stat. 182), increases chapter 1 tax by 100 percent of
   the net income attributable to an excess contribution made before the calendar year in which the
   beneficiary attains age 18. It is not listed in §26(b)(2), unlike the comparable taxes on
   Coverdell, HSA and ABLE account distributions, so as the text reads otherwise available
   nonrefundable credits could offset it. *Status: the omission is established; whether an
   exclusion was intended is a question.*
3. **A wrong cross-reference inside the Act.** §70202(c)(2) (139 Stat. 175) amends §6041 "as
   amended by section 70201(e)(1)(A)" and "(e)(1)(B)". Section 70201(e) extends the tip credit to
   beauty service businesses; the §6041 amendments are in §70201(f). OLRC executed the provision on
   probable intent. *Status: established.*
4. **Senior deduction.** §151(d)(5)(C) has no entry in §63(b), unlike the Act's other new
   non-itemizer deductions in §63(b)(5)–(7). The IRS allows it to non-itemizers and, in the 2025
   Instructions for Form 6251, treats it as a personal exemption added back for the AMT; those
   instructions cite §56(b)(5)(D), which does not exist (the provision is §56(b)(1)(D)). JCS-1-26
   n. 26 says a technical correction may be needed. *Status: question; the Form 6251 reference is
   an error.*
5. **Eleven drafting errors in the Act's text**, six of them without an OLRC editorial note, and
   **two places where the Code as published differs from the Statutes at Large** (§170(p),
   §67(g)(2)). See the table below.

## Method and limits

- **Execution.** 588 parsed edits affecting 146 sections were applied to release point 119-12,
  with occurrences counted. 566 execute exactly as written. Of the other 22, ten amend text that
  exists only after a prospective amendment by another Act and execute in their turn; eight are the
  Act's own drafting problems listed above; one (§59A(i)(2)) is resolved by a separate instruction
  in the same paragraph; three use constructions our edit model does not represent. Each has a note
  in `data/edits.csv`.
- **Comparison.** The result was compared with OLRC releases 119-33 and 119-121, and every
  difference was classified by hand.
- **Coverage.** 202 of the 216 section–provision pairs in the OLRC amendment notes are represented
  in the parsed edits. The rest are notes, References in Text, section renumberings counted under
  the former numbers, or an amendment of an earlier Act. Seven provisions with rare constructions
  were not executed automatically; for them we rely on the OLRC text.
- **Checks.** New and rewritten text was checked for repeated designations, unbalanced parentheses
  and list punctuation. Section lists that Congress usually amends together with a new provision
  were checked one by one. A blind second extraction of a random sample of 30 instructions found no
  extraction errors, after accounting for differences in the starting text.
- **Not covered.** Effective-date ordering across Acts was not modelled; prospective amendments were
  identified from OLRC notes and checked by hand. Title 31 was checked only at §1324(b).

## What we do not claim

- We do not claim that any refund, payment or assessment is unlawful. The omissions in items 1 and 2
  are textual; how the statute applies is for Congress, the Treasury, the IRS and the courts.
- We do not claim to know what Congress intended. Where the text and a likely intent differ, we say
  so and leave the question to those who can answer it.
- We do not give tax advice. No taxpayer needs to change a return because of these notes.
